How the betting works

The pool, the 4 numbers on every line, and what you actually collect — tap to read it

▾

Everyone's money goes into one pot per market. When it is decided, that pot is split between whoever backed the winner, in proportion to what they put on. Nobody takes a cut — not the organiser, not the site. Every pound in comes back out to somebody.

The 4 numbers on every line. Money on — what has already been staked on that outcome. Pays now — what the pot would pay it if it won this minute. The more money piles onto an outcome, the less it pays; the fewer people fancy it, the more. It is the pot divided by the money on that outcome, counting ante-post stakes at 1.25 as they will count at settlement — so if those two figures do not divide neatly into each other, that is why. Should pay — what the simulation reckons it ought to pay, given the handicaps and the form. Value — the gap between those two. A green plus means the pot is paying more than the outcome is worth, because not enough people have backed it yet. That is the one to look for.

None of it is a promise. Every figure moves each time anybody bets, and what you actually collect is worked out from the pot at the moment the market shuts — not the number that was on screen when you placed the bet.

Why the sums look odd on a small pot. Pays now is the market's price: the pot divided by the money already on that outcome. It is not what your next bet gets, because your own stake joins the pot and joins that outcome, which moves the price it is getting. On a thin market that is a big difference — so under each name there is a line saying what a £10 bet placed this minute would actually collect, once there is money in that market. That is the honest number; the price above it is the market, not your ticket.

And while betting is ante-post, every pound you stake counts as £1.25 when the pot is divided. So an ante-post bet collects more than the price beside it suggests — the "£10 would collect" line already has the multiplier in it.

Form book

Built from 602 individual rounds pulled from Squabbit across every player's whole history, not just the trips. Pairs-format rounds are excluded throughout, because identical scores across a pairing say nothing about either man. Nine-hole rounds count, doubled onto an 18-hole scale. 18 of the 20 have a scoring record; the other 2 have a handicap and nothing else.

Players

His average is exactly that: every 18-hole round he has ever logged on Squabbit, on a par-72 basis. Not the last 20 — all of them, right back to his first. Pairs formats are thrown out, because in foursomes and scrambles two men share one ball and the score says nothing about either of them on his own.

"Isn't it the best 8 of the last 20?" That is the handicap, and yes it is — that is how a WHS index is built. It is deliberately not how these two columns are built. A handicap is a measure of how well you can play: it takes your 8 best days out of 20 and throws the other 12 in the bin. These columns are a measure of what you will actually shoot on Thursday, which has to include the 12 in the bin. Price a golf match off everybody's best eight and you have priced a tournament nobody is playing in.

Which is also why nearly everyone's average sits above his handicap — the middle of this group is about 4 shots above, and that is completely normal rather than evidence that anyone is at it. A man is supposed to beat his handicap roughly 1 round in 4. If you want to know who is genuinely getting more shots than his golf deserves, that is what the verdict in the last column is telling you. The number behind it is the organiser's homework.

Likely in Spain is the number that matters, and it is deliberately higher than his average for nearly everybody. Marbella, La Cala Asia and Santa Maria are harder than the courses this lot play at home — those three average a slope of 135, where a gentle parkland track is nearer 120 — so the same golfer shoots more. The gap between the two columns is the course difficulty. Recent rounds count for more than old ones in working it out, so a man who has found something lately shows it.

Handicaps are the marks on each player's Squabbit profile, taken from the account that actually played the last two trips, not from whatever was keyed in after the last round. Form is a recency-weighted scoring differential, lower is better. Raw is form minus handicap straight from the data; Gap is that after thin samples are discounted towards the field median — 7 rounds should not carry the same weight as 89. Spread is day-to-day volatility.

The short version

Everything below this card is the detail. If you only read one thing, read this.

  • Every market is one pot. Everyone's money goes in; when it is decided, the pot is split between whoever backed the winner, in proportion to what they put on.
  • Nobody takes a cut. Every pound that goes in comes back out to somebody.
  • The odds are not a promise. They show how the pot would split right now. Back the outcome nobody fancies and your share is bigger; back the popular one and it is smaller.
  • Every line on your slip is a separate bet. No doubles, no trebles, no accumulators.
  • Bet early and your stake counts 1.25× when the pot divides — that is what you get for betting before the handicaps are known.
  • £10 is the most on any one line, and you can owe the pot at most £50 at a time. The second one is the one that bites: a stake cap on its own does nothing, because five tenners get to the same place as one fifty. Nobody is playing on credit and nobody is chasing anybody round a golf course. Settle up and the room reopens straight away.
  • You cannot back yourself. Markets shut 10 minutes before the relevant tee shot. Pay into the pot with the button at the top.

How the pools work

  • Every market is a tote pool. Stakes in, pool split between backers of the winning outcome in proportion to what they staked. Nothing is deducted.
  • The organiser takes no commission and holds no position against the field. A sweepstake, not a book.
  • Stakes are in whole pounds, minimum £1 and maximum £10 a line, and you can owe the pot at most £50 at a time. Back as many outcomes as you like.
  • Nobody can see your money but you. Everyone sees the size of every pot; only you see what is yours in it, and only the organiser sees the ledger. That is enforced by the database rather than by the page, so it holds however clever anyone gets with a browser.
  • You cannot back yourself, in any market, for or against. Not because anyone is a wrong 'un, but because a man with money on his own bad day is a different golfer. The Yes/No markets named after one man are shut to that man both ways. Both top-scorer markets are open to everybody: a Red man backing a Blue to top the Blues is not in that race and cannot affect it, and the awkward case — a man betting in his own team's market — is already covered by not being able to back himself. All of it is enforced by the database rather than the page, so it cannot be got round with developer tools.
  • You CAN back the other team to win overall. 25 points, 4 days, 10 a side — nobody is throwing that for a tenner, and forbidding it bought nothing but suspicion. Back Red, back Blue, back the tie. Just do not expect a warm reception when you announce it.
  • You can back more than one outcome in the same market. It is a pool, not a bookmaker, so covering 2 of the 3 team results or 2 runners in a 20-man market is an ordinary hedge and nobody is being robbed. Two things worth knowing before you do it. On a two-way market, backing both sides means you cannot lose and, unless the pool is badly lopsided, cannot really win either. And if you are the only money in a market, backing several outcomes just hands you your own stake back — the board will tell you so on the card.
  • Anyone in the group can bet, playing or not. If you are not on either team you have no restrictions at all, since there is nothing for you to influence.
  • Stakes are settled as you go. The ledger shows what you owe and the organiser marks it off when you pay. Nobody flies home owing.
  • Ante-post runs from now until the handicaps are declared on the first night. You are betting against a model built on last season's numbers and some assumptions, so stakes placed in this window count for a larger share of the pool when it divides. Betting blind should pay something.
  • Ante-post prices are deliberately flatter than the ones you will see later. The model re-draws every handicap and every form figure on each simulation to reflect that none of it is settled, so the favourite is longer and the outsider shorter than the eventual price. That is not a margin — it is the model declining to sound certain when it isn't.
  • Open runs from declaration until each market's own deadline. Handicaps are known and the prices are real, so stakes take an ordinary share. Later means safer and cheaper to judge, but a thinner slice.
  • Closed. There is no in-play betting. Every market shuts 10 minutes before the relevant tee shot — not on it. Standing on the tee watching the first drive being addressed is not a betting opportunity. Whole-trip markets — MVP, Team Result, Top Scorers, Perfect Record, The Blank — shut 10 minutes before Thursday's first ball, at 13:40 Spanish time, 12:40 UK. A round's own markets shut 10 minutes before that round tees off, so the Sunday singles book stays open until Sunday morning while Thursday's shut on Thursday.
  • It closes itself. Nobody has to remember, and the organiser cannot "forget" to shut a market he fancies. The clock runs on the tee times, and those are stored as Spanish time — so it makes no difference whether your phone is on UK time, Spanish time, or still on last year's holiday. The countdown on each market is the truth. There is a manual close in Admin as a backstop only.
  • Both phases feed the same pool on each market. The phase changes your share of it, not what you are betting on, and none of it goes to the organiser.
  • Prices are informational. Your return depends on the pool, not the model.
  • Singles only. No doubles, no trebles, no accumulators. Not out of meanness — a multiple needs the prices fixed at the moment you place it, and fixing prices is exactly the thing that makes a man a bookmaker. Every bet on this board is 1 selection into 1 pool. 20 singles are allowed and encouraged; a treble is not.

Scoring

  • 25 points at 10 a side: 5 foursomes, 5 fourball, 5 scramble, 10 singles. A win is one point, a half is a half. Change the squad size and the board recalculates; the numbers on this page follow the roster, not the other way round.
  • Red are the holders, and that makes the 2 targets different. Red won it in 2024 by 9.5 to 5.5 — under the old team names, but the same 2 sides — and retained it in 2025 on a 12 apiece tie. So this year Blue need 13 to take it and Red need only 12.5 to keep it. Anyone quoting "13 to win" at both teams is giving Red half a point they do not need. The scoreboard shows the two targets separately for exactly this reason.
  • A tie is its own outcome in the betting, whatever it does to the trophy. Team Result settles on the score: 12.5 apiece pays out on Tie, not on Red, even though Red walk home with it. Back Red and you are backing Red to outscore Blue. If you think the week ends level, back the Tie — it is the outcome that has actually happened once in two years.
  • MVP is most points contributed across the 4 sessions. Maximum 4.
  • Ties on points are settled by head-to-head singles result, then by a coin toss in the bar.

Settlement

  • The price you saw is not the price you get, and that is the point. This is a tote, not a bookmaker. There is no such thing as taking 12/1 and keeping it. The odds on screen are only the current pool divided up as it stands; when the market shuts, the pool is divided between whoever backed the winner, and everyone in that pool settles at the same rate whenever they bet. So the board moving after you bet — the model being re-run on Wednesday night, forty quid landing on your man on Thursday morning — changes nothing about your bet. It changes what the pool is worth, which is the only number that ever mattered.
  • What ante-post actually buys you. Since nobody can take a price, betting early would otherwise be a strictly worse bet: identical terms, less information. So an ante-post stake counts 1.25× when the pool divides. Stake £10 ante-post and £10 open on the same winner and the first counts as £12.50 against the second's £10. That is the whole compensation for betting blind, and it comes out of the other backers' share, not out of anybody's pocket — the pool is still divided whole.
  • Worked example. £100 in the MVP pool, made up of lots of tenners and fivers. £40 of it went on the winner ante-post, £20 on the winner once the marks were agreed, and £40 on men who did not win. Winning shares are 40 × 1.25 = 50, plus 20, so 70 in total. The ante-post backers hold 50 of those 70 shares and take £71.43 of the pool; the open backers hold 20 and take £28.57. Nothing is left over, because nothing is taken out.
  • Dead heat. Pool splits equally between tied outcomes, then between backers within each.
  • Nobody wins the market. It can happen: The Blank pays the man who scores nothing and everybody scores something; Perfect Record pays a four-from-four and nobody manages it. There is no house here — nobody is on the other side of these bets, the backers are betting each other, so an unwon pool has nothing to divide and nobody to divide it to. It does not become the organiser's money. Not ever, not once, not "for his trouble".
  • Nobody won it: money back. If the market simply has no winner — everybody scored, nobody went four-from-four — every stake is refunded in full. That needs no decision from anybody; the board does it. A refund is your own money back, so it comes back at face value: £10 placed ante-post is £10 returned, not £12.50. The 1.25 is a share of a pool, and there is no pool to have a share of.
  • Somebody won it and nobody backed him: the 2027 pot. Rarer, and the only case where money is left over. A winner exists but not a penny was on him, so there is nobody to pay. That money rolls into next year's kitty, read out and declared in the ledger before anyone leaves. It is the group's money, never the organiser's. Would you rather have your stake back instead? Say so before the flight home and you get it, in full, without comment.
  • Non-runner. Anyone who does not tee it up at all is refunded in full on every market naming them. Stakes on other outcomes stand.
  • Except where not turning up is the bet. Rob's 30th has Does not make the tee at all as one of its four outcomes, so if Rob fails to appear that market does not void — it settles, and whoever backed it collects. You cannot be refunded on a market for the exact thing it was written to pay out on. The same applies to any future market where the non-appearance is itself a named outcome.
  • Withdrawal mid-trip. Points already banked stand; missed sessions score zero. Backers are not refunded — you backed a man who might get injured.
  • Abandoned session. That session's matches are halved and its markets settle on the half.
  • Scoring record. Squabbit is the record. If Squabbit and a paper card disagree, Squabbit wins.
  • Handicaps are agreed in the bar on the first night, locked the moment they are declared, and not revisited whatever happens on Thursday. Yes, even if he shoots the round of his life. Especially then.
  • Novelty markets are settled by the organiser on the evidence available. Video beats testimony.
  • Disputes go to the organiser. Any dispute involving the organiser goes to the 2 captains; if they cannot agree the pool is voided and refunded, and everyone sulks equally.
  • "I was robbed" is not a settlement mechanism. Neither is "the wind got up".

Private betting between friends is lawful in the UK and winnings are untaxed. What would change that is the organiser taking a cut or building a margin — that is bookmaking and needs a licence. So the organiser never takes a cut — not a percentage, not a rounding, not a pound out of a pool nobody won. Money that goes unclaimed belongs to the group and rolls into next year's trip, and anyone can ask for his stake back out of it up to the flight home.

Admin

Roster, handicaps, teams, courses, payment link, settlement and the pool ledger. The organiser is let straight in on his sign-in and never needs the passphrase. Anybody else needs both a signed-in account and the passphrase, because the database refuses every one of these functions to a caller with no session — so a passphrase on its own gets you a screen full of buttons that do nothing.

This passphrase is checked by the database, not by this page. It is never written to this device, and it is the only thing standing between someone and the shared book — voiding bets, recording payments, publishing handicaps and opening or closing the betting all go through it. Wrong guesses are slowed down at the database, which makes working through them hopeless.

Everyone else can read the book and place their own bets. Nobody but the organiser can change one.

The pot — where the money actually is

The board deals in stakes. Your bank deals in cash. These are not the same number until everyone has paid, and this is the line that tells you which. Should be in the account is the only figure you can check against Starling.

Settle the markets

Call each market as it is decided. Pick the winning outcome — or more than one for a dead heat — or mark it void if nobody won it at all. Everything after that is arithmetic: the pool divides itself, ante-post stakes carry their multiplier, and each man sees his own return and nobody else's. Nothing is deducted anywhere.

A settled market stops taking bets immediately, on every phone and in the database, so nobody can back a result that has already been called. Unsettle puts it back to pending if you call one wrong. Withdraw it pulls a market off the board without settling it — use that for one that turns out to be a bad idea, then settle it void to hand the money back.

Who to pay

The moment a market is settled, everyone owed money appears here. Work down the list, hand the money over, and press Paid as you go — the row goes green and drops out of the outstanding total, so you can put the phone down mid-round and pick up exactly where you left off.

Settle up

What each man staked, what he has paid in, what he has collected, and the difference. Positive means he is up and the pot owes him; negative means he owes the pot.

Ledger

Every bet anyone has placed, on any phone. This view is yours alone — punters can only see their own bets and the size of each pot. Tap the button to record someone as settled up.

Every bet

Void anything placed by mistake. Voided bets leave the pools and the ledger immediately but stay in the database, so there is always a record of what happened — and they are listed below, where you can put one back if you voided the wrong line. A market that has already been settled has to be unsettled first, in both directions: the pool was worked out on those bets.

Voided

Placed and then taken off. None of this money is in a pool or on anybody's balance. If a man paid before you voided his bets he is now in credit, and the ledger above says so.

Payment

Starling Settle Up is the tool: it produces a secure link, Starling users pay in the app and everyone else pays by debit card on a web page, so nobody needs to be a customer. Create it once, paste it here, and it shows as a Pay button in the header, on every tab. Money lands in your main account — sweep it into a Chip & Sip Space yourself, because Spaces cannot be paid into from outside.

Roster

Change a handicap and every price moves. Form is expected scoring differential, pre-filled from history — nudge it if someone has clearly changed. Players marked form assumed have a handicap but no logged rounds, so their scoring is the field-median assumption rather than anything measured.

Accounts

Who has signed up and which name they claimed. Deleting an account frees the name so the sign-up can be run again — useful for testing, and for when somebody claims the wrong man. Bets are kept by default, because they are recorded against a name rather than a login, so re-registering that name picks them straight back up. Tick the box first if you want them voided too.

Handicap declaration

Bets taken before the first night are ante-post: placed while the handicaps are still guesses. Type the agreed marks into the Roster on the Squad tab and press Apply, re-run and publish, then come back here and press Declare handicaps — every price moves and the board reprices itself. Both switches below ask you to confirm, and both can be put straight back.

Blind uncertainty is how much the model widens its prices while handicaps are undeclared. Every simulation draws each player a slightly different handicap and form — about a shot either way for the men with a scoring record, considerably more for the two without. Favourites drift, longshots come in, and nothing pretends to a precision it has not got. Declare the handicaps and it switches off, so the board sharpens the moment you know something. Set it to 0 to disable.

The book shuts itself, market by market, 10 minutes before each round tees off — the last of them on Sunday morning, so the singles stay backable all week. You do not have to remember. Close it early if you want.

A tote has no concept of taking a price: everyone in a pool gets the same terms whenever they bet, so without this an ante-post bet is simply a worse bet — same return, less information. The multiplier fixes that by giving early money a larger share of the same pool. At 1.25 a pound staked blind counts as £1.25 when the pool is divided. It moves money between backers rather than to the organiser, so there is still no rake. Set it to 1 to switch the whole idea off.

Courses and format

Model prices

Monte Carlo of the full Ryder Cup: foursomes, fourball, scramble and singles, hole by hole, with any announced pairing played as it stands and the rest redrawn every simulation, so an undrawn price averages over who might end up with whom instead of guessing at it.

Novelty markets

The model cannot price these. Add outcomes, take stakes, settle by eye.

Something worth telling the group

The board watches for the 4 things people actually want to hear about — a market about to shut, a session finished, the pot passing a round number, and a winner still waiting to be paid. Anything it has spotted since you last looked is below, written as a message short enough that people will read it on a phone. Share hands it to your phone's share sheet, where WhatsApp is the first icon; Copy is the fallback on a laptop.

Backups

The whole book — every stake, payment, payout, result, the roster, the card and the settings — is snapshotted every night at 02:30, automatically, by the database itself. Nothing has to be running and nobody has to remember. The last forty are kept.

This is insurance against the mistake that actually happens: a market settled on the wrong man, or a roster edit that takes a name off mid-week. Take one now before anything you are nervous about. Download puts a copy on your phone, which is the only version that survives the database itself going away — do that once on the Sunday night.

Morning report

The message for the group. Score, who still owes, and the biggest pools, in plain text with no columns — WhatsApp will not keep them lined up. Anyone who has ticked already paid is listed separately rather than chased, so nobody gets named in front of 20 people for a payment that is already sitting in your account.

Dress rehearsal

Run the whole Sunday-night sequence on obviously fake money before it matters. This drops five bets of £1 to £10 into a market of your choosing, under names beginning REHEARSAL so they can never be mistaken for real ones. Settle it below, check the payouts and the settle-up sheet add up, then clear it. Clearing removes the fake bets and any settlement they caused, and nothing else — a real bet or a real result in the same market survives untouched.

Save your state

The book itself lives in the database — every stake, the ledger and the published prices. This is only your local copy of the roster, courses and settings. Show it before a risky edit, paste it back to undo one.

What phones are hitting

If the board misbehaves on somebody's phone it says so here, by itself. Nobody has to describe the problem down a bad line, and nobody can read this but you — the phones can write to the list and cannot see a word of it. Identical faults are grouped, so 40 of the same thing is one line saying 40. An empty list is the answer you want.

Clear the book for launch

Empties the money and the results and leaves the setup standing. Run it once, the moment before the link goes to the group, so nobody opens a board carrying your testing. It takes a snapshot first, which appears in Backups above, and every phone drops its own half-built slip within 45 seconds.

What goes: every bet, every payment and payout, every "I have sent it" note, every settled market, and anything written on the card. What stays: the players and their teams, the men who have already claimed their name, who has admin, the declared handicaps, the published prices and the payment link.
